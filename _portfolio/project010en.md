In organising every event, I have always wrestled with the same attachment: the hope that each gathering will give its participants a singular experience, a different feeling or insight. In pursuit of this “perfect meaning”, I repeatedly deliberate over themes, search everywhere for the right venue, polish every stage of the programme and devise ways to make each meeting valuable, rewarding and memorable.

Yet an excessive pursuit of perfection, and an excessive insistence upon meaning, became the greatest constraint of all. Long preparations, recurring self-doubt and constant attempts to predict the outcome often caused events that had once filled me with anticipation to be postponed beneath layer after layer of concern, then reluctantly abandoned. For a long time, I remained trapped in this cycle: trying to assign a clear purpose to every gathering, only to miss the gathering itself through my relentless pursuit of purpose.

Our previous event once again exceeded my expectations in both length and content. It also shattered my rigid assumptions about how an event should be run and inspired a bold experiment. This time, I set no predetermined rules or theme. I did not prepare discussion prompts or manufacture an atmosphere; I even did away with registration. Everyone was free to arrive as they wished and join at any time. Those who wanted to talk could speak freely about daily trifles and passing emotions. Those who did not could sit quietly, feel the evening breeze and let their thoughts drift. No one needed to accommodate another person or force themselves to participate. This unstructured, unhurried gathering taught me that when we do not know what meaning to give an event, the empty space itself may be the finest meaning.

This difficulty extends far beyond organising events. Our entire lives are too easily imprisoned by the word “meaning”. From childhood onwards, we seem to inhabit a single system of value, carried forward by one uniform standard of what counts as worthwhile. At school, we spend countless hours bent over our books, yet if our effort does not produce impressive marks, everyone—including ourselves—instinctively judges those days and nights to have been meaningless. Once at work, we labour diligently and give everything we have, hurrying to make a living. But if this does not bring generous rewards or conspicuous achievement, years of perseverance are casually dismissed as wasted effort.

The world continually supplies standard answers for a meaningful life: fame, success, promotion and abundant results. It is as though our efforts, experiences and very existence possess value only when they satisfy a conventional definition of success. Yet on reflection, this single prescribed meaning is itself the greatest contradiction. If everyone entered the same race, pursued the same form of success and arrived at the same supposed perfection, would lives made identical still possess any genuine meaning?

All beauty and value arise through difference and contrast. Some people adore the radiance and heat of midsummer. But if summer were the only season—without the gentleness of spring, the melancholy of autumn or the stillness of winter—its warmth would become monotonous and lose what makes it precious. Life is the same. Not every experience must lead to success, every effort produce a return, or every gathering deliver growth. Quiet days, unguarded moments and intervals of doing nothing—companionship and conversation without an ulterior purpose—are valuable experiences in themselves.

We spend our lives pursuing a template designed by society, struggling to satisfy external judgement and fastening ourselves into the demand that everything must be useful, produce results and carry meaning. We exhaust ourselves and grow anxious, gradually losing touch with what we truly feel. In chasing the world's meaning, we lose the rhythm of our own lives.

The true value of this “meaningless” conversation lay precisely in its lack of meaning. It taught me that life need not be perpetually tense, purposeful or productive. An idle hour, a spontaneous conversation or a stretch of apparently useless time is not life wasted. It is part of healing and accepting ourselves.

The meaning of life has never been found in reproducing someone else's success or conforming to society's standards. It lies in breaking free from a uniform system of value and discovering our own rhythm and way of living. We may allow ourselves to spend time without purpose, accept that effort does not always bring reward, welcome the ordinary and the unremarkable, and live with uncertainty. In a life spacious enough to breathe, we can notice its small beauties and inhabit a way of being that belongs to us alone. That may be life's most precious and singular meaning.

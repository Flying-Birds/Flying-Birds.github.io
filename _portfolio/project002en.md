Pope Francis died on 21 April 2025. That evening, I came across many posts on Xiaohongshu from people asking for help. That was how this charitable peer support group began. I know I may never attain the spiritual stature of Francis, yet I still long to offer what little light I have: to shoulder a measure of social responsibility in my finite life and kindle a warm lamp for someone else.

I began by trying to form a support group on Xiaohongshu. One person’s strength is limited, but I was fortunate to meet Fahe, Yanyan, Cheese, Wanting and others at the very beginning. They offered ideas as soon as they joined, and their steadfast support dispelled the uncertainty of those first steps. The arrival of old friends was equally heartening. Organising events is never without setbacks, but their support made every step surer.

I owe particular thanks to Wanting. From contacting venues to shaping the event, he quietly supported this fledgling group throughout. Without his enthusiasm, many things might have stalled, and frustration might have persuaded me to give up. When I was recently struggling to plan our next event, he volunteered to give a talk; that offer made this gathering possible.

It was a joy to meet so many new friends in person, and I look forward to the day our online members can join us offline. The event was scheduled from one to three: an hour on the book and an hour to catch up. Before arriving, Xiao He asked, “Is Wanting going to speak for two hours?” I said that was impossible—his slides numbered only twelve or thirteen, so one hour should be plenty. In fact, he spoke for nearly five hours. It was after half past six when we finished. His subject was compelling and his eloquence exceptional; holding everyone’s full attention for five hours was genuinely impressive. (I had better stop praising him, or his feet may never touch the ground again.) I listened closely and took two full pages of notes, only to realise at home that I could not extract a single practical step from them. I decided to be kind to myself and leave professional matters to the professionals. What follows is simply a scattering of personal reflections.

After primary school, I travelled to Dalian with my parents. A guide took us to a temple, where a fortune-telling monk said, “You are very likely to study abroad one day.” His prophecy fell into my heart like a seed. Years later, when I held an overseas offer in my hands, the seed suddenly broke through the soil. Had destiny left a clue all along? Were the late nights at my desk, the sweat spent improving my grades, and the steps I kept taking through uncertainty merely meaningless footnotes in a script already written? The question wound itself around the years that followed: at every crossroads, I wondered where fate ended and effort began.

By the end of this event, that tangled vine had begun to loosen, and an answer emerged in its new leaves.

Understanding destiny may be like studying a contour map before climbing a mountain. We learn where the slopes are steep and the crossings dangerous so that we can carry a walking stick, wear shoes with better grip, and know when to rest and when to persevere—not so that we may sit at the foot of the mountain and lament. The “contour map” hidden within fate never marks a fixed destination. It helps us choose the ground beneath our feet with clearer eyes.

The most moving things in life are rarely miracles that appear from nowhere. They are the quiet acts of persistence woven through ordinary days: giving everything despite knowing that the reward may never come. A spring flower blooms with all its strength although its season lasts barely a fortnight—not to defeat its fading, but to unfurl every petal while it can. A winter tree drives its roots deep into frozen soil although the wind will take its final leaf—not to deny winter, but to be the first to catch the warming breeze when the snow melts. Such persistence may be our gentlest answer to fate: rather than ask whether the road has already been written, we place each present step firmly upon it.

In difficult times, the “possibilities” marked out by fate resemble a blurred treasure map. It cannot draw the exact path, but it offers a glimmer through the fog. It does not invite you to lie down and wait for treasure to find you; it whispers, “Walk this way, and perhaps you will discover something.” So you cross the mud, rise and rub your knees after falling, drink from a mountain spring when thirsty, and finally see dawn spill across the summit. Only then do you understand: the map was never meant to dictate where you must arrive. It gave you the courage to begin. Effort is not a rebellion against fate; it is the way we give the journey deeper meaning once we glimpse where fate might lead.

On the final slide of Wanting’s presentation stood a single line, his gift to everyone present: “Among the countless paths of fate, may you live without regret.”

A faint fragrance of locust blossom drifted through the window, as though softly answering him. The flowers never fail their season; once they bloom, they leave the path fragrant. Every assured step we take will likewise shape a landscape uniquely ours among fate’s countless roads.

May every step carry you toward what your heart desires, and may the life you have travelled leave you without regret.

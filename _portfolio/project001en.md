The ties that bring people together can be wonderfully mysterious.

<div style="text-align: center;">
  <img src="assets/img/portfolio/01-content-02.jpg" alt="" style="max-width: 80%; height: auto;">
</div>

That day, Beijing was caught in a torrential downpour. Raindrops struck the windows hard, blurring the view until even the road a few dozen metres ahead appeared only in fragments. My phone kept lighting up, but most messages said the same thing: “The rain is too heavy. I’m staying home today.” With each cancellation, my own resolve weakened. My finger hovered over the chat box as I wondered whether to message the only person besides my friend who had said he would definitely come, and suggest cancelling the support-group gathering altogether.

Yet I paused, and never pressed send. I remembered the determination with which I had founded the group. I had known from the beginning that the road would not always be smooth, and I had made myself a quiet promise: if even one person needed this gathering, I would be there. I took a deep breath, carefully did my makeup, put on my favourite navy shirt—as though observing a private ritual—then opened my umbrella and stepped into the rain.

My unease stayed with me along the way. I may be a consummate extrovert, but I am oddly afraid of breaking the ice; with strangers, I often rely on the Northeasterner’s ingrained sense of social duty to keep a conversation alive. I was about to meet an online acquaintance I had barely spoken to, so a little apprehension was inevitable. From the few details he had mentioned—formerly in HR, now studying Buddhism and offering destiny consultations—I assembled a picture in my mind: a neat crew cut, an average build, white meditation robes, prayer beads around his wrist, and the composed manner of someone who had weathered life’s rises and falls, speaking unhurriedly about fate.

The rain continued, an unbroken curtain beyond the car window. Its steady drumming carried my thoughts farther and farther as I tried to imagine the stranger I was about to meet. Only when the driver said, “We’re here,” did I return to the present.

I stepped out and picked my way through puddles in the hutong. The sign of Penghao Theatre glowed warmly through the rain. I told the receptionist, “I’m here to run an event. Wanting booked the venue.” I followed the direction of her gesture towards the back room. A young man in combat boots walked out. With delicate features, clear pale skin and a slender, almost fragile frame, he looked like a boy. He met my eyes with a bright gaze and said softly, “I’m Wanting.”

For a moment, I simply stared, my umbrella still dripping in my hand. He was nothing like the person I had imagined.

The chairs in the back room were cool to the touch. We introduced ourselves, then candidly confessed to each other that neither of us had actually read the book. We both burst out laughing, and the awkwardness vanished. Our conversation wandered from the purpose of the support group to our lives, from the Buddhist idea of affinity to amusing stories from work. Before we knew it, three hours had passed. Outside, the rain stopped and started again; inside, neither of us noticed the time.

Eventually I sighed. “It feels like a failure. My first event, this enormous storm, and hardly anyone came.”

Wanting answered with complete sincerity: “Perhaps heaven is helping you choose. It kept away the people who were never on your wavelength, the people you were not meant to meet, and left space for those who truly belonged here.”

“Then why did you come?” I asked.

He looked at me, his expression unwavering. “Because I promised you. A promise should be kept. Even if you had not come, I still would have.”

Something stirred in me. I smiled. “Then fate must have meant for us to meet.”

Looking back, had I not honoured my responsibility to hold the gathering even for one person, and had he not honoured his promise to come, perhaps we would have remained strangers who occasionally liked each other’s social-media posts. We would never have become the close friends who later spoke about everything.

When the cancellations arrived, my heart had sunk. For a moment, the road ahead disappeared from view and even the conviction that had set me on it began to fail. Wanting’s arrival was like a sudden beam of light through the rain. His words—“I promised you”—slowly warmed my chilled heart. In that moment I understood that, whatever difficulties awaited the support group, at least one person would stand beside me. I no longer had to forge ahead alone.

I have always considered myself fortunate. At the hardest moments, heaven seems to send someone to steady me. I keep a list of seventeen names: all the benefactors I have met in more than thirty years of life. They appeared when I was lost or vulnerable, illuminating the road ahead with a word or a gesture.

The next time I saw Wanting was at our second event, where he read my destiny chart.

What he knew was that I would meet many benefactors in this life. What he did not know was that his name had already been added to the list.

Some people find a place in your heart the moment they brave the pouring rain to keep a promise.

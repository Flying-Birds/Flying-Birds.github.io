Warmth travelled on the breeze and flowers adorned the spring branches. With Grain Rain upon us, the tenderness of spring was quietly drawing to a close. While the season still lingered, we gathered in Dongfeng Park for our ninth event. Leaving behind the noise and tension of the city, we began a restorative journey into self-awareness.

<div style="text-align: center;">
  <img src="assets/img/portfolio/09-content-02.jpg" alt="" style="max-width: 80%; height: auto;">
</div>

Much of our inner conflict, anxiety, sensitivity and emotional exhaustion arises, at its root, from a lack of self-awareness. For too long, we are carried along by outside voices, habitual patterns of thought and other people's judgements. Outwardly, we maintain the image of someone composed and mature, bending ourselves to social expectations and the gaze of others. Inwardly, we suppress fatigue and difficult emotions, repeatedly condemning every imperfection. We instinctively search outside ourselves for answers, dwelling on troubled relationships and life's minor frustrations, yet rarely pause to look within. Unable to recognise our deepest needs or distinguish immediate emotion from prejudice embedded in the subconscious, we become trapped in rigid cycles of thought and consume ourselves over and over. This is why so many people live in a state of lasting physical and emotional strain.

We must first correct a misunderstanding: self-awareness is not overthinking, relentless self-examination or self-criticism, nor does it mean magnifying every flaw. Genuine self-awareness means stepping beyond our subjective viewpoint and observing ourselves with the neutrality of a witness: noticing our changing emotions, hidden attachments, fixed habits and fundamental needs. It asks us neither to judge nor condemn, neither to resist vulnerability nor reject sadness, but to accept fully this imperfect yet complete self. It is the foundation of emotional healing and mental growth—one of the hardest, yet most essential, capacities of adult life.

Fortunately, the ability to look within and recognise our true heart is not an inborn gift. It is a skill we can weave into everyday life and gradually learn through three steps.

First, notice the rise and fall of emotion. When anxiety, irritation, hurt or resistance appears, pause before acting or reacting impulsively. Stop arguing with yourself. Rather than ask, “Why am I being so oversensitive?”, simply acknowledge your present state. Are you exhausted, lacking a sense of safety, or resisting external pressure? Recognising that an emotion exists is the first step towards awareness.

Second, notice the underlying thought beneath the emotion. Many surface feelings are outward expressions of deeper attachments. Anger and sensitivity may conceal insecurity formed early in life. Avoiding social contact may arise from a fear of rejection. The habit of forcing oneself to appear strong often rests upon the rigid belief that vulnerability is unacceptable. Self-awareness means looking through the surface emotion to find the assumptions and subconscious beliefs driving it, and distinguishing what truly belongs to us from what has been imposed by society or conditioned by our surroundings.

Finally, notice our patterns of behaviour. Observe the logic behind habitual avoidance, people-pleasing, self-attack and excessive displays of strength. Recognise when inertia is carrying us forward. Only by seeing these unconscious patterns do we gain the opportunity to leave the closed loop of old thinking and gradually change the behaviours that exhaust us.

Self-improvement without awareness remains only superficial consolation. Those who cannot face their vulnerability and inner attachments cannot achieve genuine reconciliation with themselves. Only by seeing clearly the limits of our understanding, our emotional sensitivities and our deepest fixations can we loosen the chains of emotion and habitual thought and regain agency over our lives.

Yet self-awareness is not a solitary discipline. We are all vulnerable to being blinded by our own position, caught in closed loops of self-perception from which it is difficult to escape alone. Gentle companionship with kindred people, reciprocal listening and deep empathy provide invaluable support. At each gathering, we recognise ourselves in another person's experience. The objective perspective of a compassionate observer can reveal our blind spots, help us accept our imperfections and ease the uncertainty and inner conflict that arise when reflecting alone.

Grain Rain passed and spring began to fade, but the discipline of looking within does not end with a season. This gentle spring gathering reminded us that the finest growth is not an outward chase or a demand that we become invulnerable. It is the quiet work of deepening our roots within and knowing ourselves clearly.

Rereading *The Surrender Experiment*, I was struck by the author's decision to return to ordinary life for spiritual practice. By tempering himself amid everyday concerns, he gained a more lucid understanding of both growth and life. Practice does not require us to seek tranquillity in distant mountains or ancient temples. Amid the bustle and minutiae of ordinary existence, we can see our emotions more honestly and understand the logic beneath them. Bringing self-awareness into every meal and season—neither trapped by changing emotions nor confined by rigid prejudice, accepting our true nature, releasing attachment and treating ourselves gently—is the most grounded and powerful practice of all.

May we preserve this attentive heart, taking root within while growing calmly into the world. May we reflect clearly in solitude and nourish one another in kindred company; and for all the years ahead, may we make peace with ourselves, support those who share our wavelength and grow steadily together.

In an age carried along by fragmented information and beset by restlessness and inner conflict, reading offers one of the finest ways to converse with ourselves and make peace with the world. At this book club, Long Shaonian drew upon three works—*Cognitive Awakening*, *Mindfulness of Breathing* and *One Sentence Is Worth Ten Thousand*—to connect deeper reflections on self-knowledge, awareness of mind and body, and human connection. The books focus respectively on cognition, mindfulness practice and the texture of ordinary human life. Their subjects appear very different, yet a single path of growth runs through them: accepting our limitations, cultivating inner clarity and moving towards sincere encounters with others.

*Cognitive Awakening* overturns many of our fixed assumptions about the self. We imagine that our thoughts and choices arise from our authentic will, when in fact we are often carried along by the primitive instincts of the brain. Human society changes at extraordinary speed, but the brain evolves far more slowly. Survival patterns formed millions of years ago still govern contemporary behaviour and thought. The limitations of human nature described in the book correspond closely to the Buddhist “three poisons”—greed, aversion and delusion—and reveal, layer by layer, the roots of ordinary inner conflict.

“Greed” is our attachment to immediate satisfaction, comfort and external approval. We crave ease and pleasure, lose ourselves in fleeting gratification, avoid difficulty and procrastinate when life becomes demanding. Instincts once essential to survival have become chains of self-consumption. “Aversion” is the instinctive counterattack that follows emotional overwhelm. Fear, anxiety and anger evolved to protect us, yet we often become their servants: arguing, brooding and acting impulsively, speaking unwisely or choosing badly in the heat of emotion, then falling into endless regret.

Most hidden, and hardest to overcome, is the “delusion” rooted in the subconscious. Our family of origin, formative experiences and the judgements of others leave invisible impressions within us and become the fixed lens through which we view the world. We assume that what we see is reality and what we think is our true mind, yet we continually interpret life through prejudice and stereotype. Many people know this predicament. Labelled aloof, introverted or difficult as children, they gradually acquire a powerful habit of self-rejection. Faced with people or situations they dislike, they cannot calmly accept their feelings and instead attack themselves. Before elders or authority, they habitually hesitate to ask, seek help or believe themselves worthy of kindness. Such subconscious attachment is particularly dangerous because it disguises itself as self-knowledge, quietly confines a life and consumes a person's strength through constant inner struggle.

Recognising our limitations is the first step of awakening. *Mindfulness of Breathing* offers a practical discipline for loosening attachment and cultivating clarity. It is not abstract mysticism, but a grounded method of training awareness of mind and body. Its purpose is not to force every stray thought away or compel ourselves to remain calm. It uses the breath as an anchor through which we develop the perspective of an observer. When thoughts surge and emotions rise, we need neither resist nor become anxious. We simply notice them, then gently return our attention to the present.

Anyone who meditates knows how difficult consistency can be. The mind wanders, becoming absorbed in regret for the past or rehearsing anxiety about the future, unable to settle in the present. With continued practice, however, we gradually understand that the world's joys and sorrows, gains and losses, and every rise and fall are transient appearances. The power of awareness can address greed, aversion and delusion directly. When we crave comfort, we can perceive the emptiness beneath the craving. When emotion takes control, we can sense the tension in mind and body. When we reject ourselves, we can recognise the rigid prejudice at work. Mindful awareness enables us to step beyond the pull of desire and emotion and restore a living balance between reason and our authentic nature. Practice is never accomplished overnight. Clarity may be easier in solitude than composure among other people, but each moment of awareness carries a small power to break an old pattern and heal the self.

We practise inwardly to settle the mind and body, yet search outwardly for resonance. *One Sentence Is Worth Ten Thousand* gives voice to the loneliness and longing hidden in the human heart. We are rarely without company: meals, conversation and shared journeys are common. Yet people with whom we can truly speak, empathise and meet heart to heart are scarce. This loneliness is not the sadness of physical isolation, but the burden of feelings that no one understands, receives or shares.

The distance between people arises largely because each of us carries our own attachments and prejudices. When others speak, we often interpret their words through our personal filters and projections rather than listening with sincerity. Genuine connection is therefore immeasurably precious: one sentence that truly understands us is worth ten thousand polite but empty exchanges. This is also the purpose of our book club. In an age of silence and inner conflict, it creates a corner of sincerity where we can set down our disguises and prejudices, speak openly and listen gently. Here we exchange thoughts, help one another heal and ease the loneliness of solitude through the resonance of kindred minds.

Three readings offered three forms of growth and three kinds of clarity. *Cognitive Awakening* helped us recognise the limits of human nature and understand the roots of inner conflict. *Mindfulness of Breathing* offered a path of practice through which awareness can settle mind and body. *One Sentence Is Worth Ten Thousand* revealed the warmth of human life and the preciousness of sincere connection. Reading is never merely the accumulation of knowledge. It is a discipline of deepening within and growing into the world.

Preparing and reflecting upon this book-sharing session was itself an opportunity for review and inner consolidation. In arranging our thoughts, we deepen understanding and allow what matters to settle. In future, may we continue to take reading as our vessel and awareness as our sail, freeing ourselves from attachment and inner conflict while preserving clarity and gentleness. May we gather strength through growth and warmth through sincere encounters—untroubled by stray thoughts, unconfined by prejudice, at ease in ourselves and always growing towards the sun.

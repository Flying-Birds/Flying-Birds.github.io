What is love?

Lao Kan shared a delightful story. One day, several young colleagues were discussing love when a woman in her fifties recalled: “Thirty years ago, I saw my future husband carrying a radio. He wore bell-bottoms and sunglasses, with a folding fan tucked behind his neck and a cigarette between his lips. I thought the way he smoked was impossibly handsome. I was completely captivated.” That was how their love began.

Perhaps every love story has such a sweet beginning: smiling foolishly at old messages on the sofa, feeling one’s heart race after an accidental meeting of eyes, sensing sweetness in the very air whenever the other person comes to mind. First attraction is a firework flowering in the night—brilliant and ardent, illuminating an ordinary life.

The second half of the story? Thirty years later, whenever she sees her husband smoking at home, she wants to “finish him off.”

When the first blaze of passion fades into the minutiae of daily life, we slowly forget how the other person first appeared to us. Dissatisfaction and criticism creep in. We argue over who washes the dishes or takes out the rubbish, and refuse to yield over whether toothpaste should be squeezed from the middle or the end. Resentment becomes fine grit hidden inside love’s shoes.

Yet it is precisely through these stumbles that we learn acceptance.

We come to understand that no two people are identical. Different families and different paths of growth make each of us unique. He has his habits; I have my convictions. We stop demanding that the other person change. Between coffee and tea, we learn to order a pot of hot water; after an argument, we still leave a light on to welcome each other home.

Through repeated conflict and reconciliation, we also learn to communicate and understand.

We allow what is “bottled up” to become what is “spoken aloud.” Instead of accusing, “Why don’t you understand me?” we say, “I feel a little sad right now.” We stop fighting over who is right and who is wrong. We sit on the same side and face the problem as our common adversary.

An honest conversation over dinner, or a walk taken without looking at our phones, can dissolve countless misunderstandings. Silence after work is not necessarily indifference; it may simply be exhaustion. Carelessness is not always a lack of concern; life’s pressures may have left someone struggling to breathe. Nagging may be worry in disguise. We learn to leave each other a key to the door of the heart.

And when life’s storms arrive, we learn to hold one another up.

Love is the hand that tucks the blanket around you late at night, the glass of warm water brought when you are ill, and every hardship crossed together over the long years. It is no longer a grand declaration, but the figure who stands unwaveringly beside you when you need them most.

Neither life nor love is always smooth. True love is not the ability to preserve forever the thrill of a first meeting. It is choosing, after wind and rain, to keep holding the other person’s hand and walk on together.

It begins with the splendour of fireworks, then settles amid meals, chores and ordinary days into a quiet, enduring warmth. Acceptance, communication, understanding and support bind two independent hearts together, transforming “I” into “we.”

So if you ask me what love is, my answer is this:

It is the flutter of a first encounter, and the peace of long companionship.

It is the honing of my sharp edges into our unspoken harmony.

Nothing more—and nothing less.
